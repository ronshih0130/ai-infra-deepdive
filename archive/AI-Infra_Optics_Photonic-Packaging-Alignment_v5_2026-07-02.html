Executive snapshot

為什麼是現在

The AI-optics boom has re-priced almost everything that emits light — lasers, PICs, DSPs, modules ran +100% to +1,700%. What the crowd has under-owned is the unglamorous assembly step: coupling light between a laser, a chip and a hair-thin fiber to a tolerance finer than a wavelength, then testing it. It is the single biggest slice of a photonic device's cost, it is still manual, and capacity is running 40–60% behind demand. Yet — the honest catch — the firm you can actually buy to own that step (Fabrinet) earns a ~12% gross margin even while turning demand away. Tightness is real; the rent is captured somewhere else.

◆ Explain it simply · 白話版

Think of an optical transceiver as a tiny lighthouse. Making the light (the laser chip) is hard — earlier reports covered that. But once you have the light, you still have to aim it perfectly down a glass fiber thinner than a hair, glue everything in place so it never moves, and test that the beam actually arrives. Aim it wrong by a millionth of a metre and the signal is lost. That aiming-and-gluing step is called optical packaging / alignment / fiber-attach. It's slow, still mostly done by machines-with-humans, and it eats 70–80% of the cost of the whole device.

As AI needs tens of millions more of these per year — and as the industry moves to co-packaged optics (CPO), where the optics sit right next to the switch chip and packaging gets ~2× harder — this aiming step becomes the traffic jam. Classic chokepoint: everything downstream must pass through it.

The catch (and the whole point of this report): being the traffic jam doesn't mean you collect the toll. The company you can buy to do the assembly (Fabrinet) makes razor-thin margins even while sold out — it earns volume, not pricing power. The real profit either moves up to the foundry (TSMC's COUPE, ~62% margins) for CPO, or gets deleted by a new "passive alignment" trick (POET) that skips the aiming altogether. So this is a report about a genuine chokepoint where you have to think carefully about who keeps the money.

L3 — components: the parts that go in — the PIC (silicon-photonics chip), the laser die, the fiber, micro-lenses, the fiber-array unit (FAU).
L2 — sub-assembly (the chokepoint): aligning + attaching those parts to sub-micron precision, then optical test. The "last millimeter."
L1 — finished product: the sealed pluggable transceiver (800G/1.6T) or the co-packaged optical engine bolted next to a switch ASIC.
Upstream / tools: the alignment machines, die-bonders and optical testers (ficonTEC, PI, Aerotech, K&S, MKS/Newport) that gate how fast L2 can run.
Active alignment
Aligning fiber-to-chip with live optical-power feedback — highest precision, but slow & serial (one device at a time). The status-quo bottleneck.
Passive alignment
Using pre-etched features/fixtures so parts snap into place — cheaper & faster, historically less precise. What POET's interposer and photonic-wire-bonding chase.
CPO
Co-packaged optics — the optical engine moves onto the same package as the switch/GPU chip. Deletes pluggable content but makes packaging ~2× harder (thousands of fibers, no hot-swap).
Fiber-attach / FAU
Bonding the optical fiber (often via a fiber-array unit) to the photonic chip's edge or surface — the physically hardest, lowest-yield step, over 6 degrees of freedom.
OSAT / COUPE
OSAT = outsourced assembly & test house (ASE, Amkor). COUPE = TSMC's silicon-photonics packaging platform that pulls CPO assembly into the foundry — and its margins.
Rent capture
Who actually keeps the scarcity profit. A link can be tight (a chokepoint) yet capture little rent if the buyer or a neighbouring link holds the pricing power. Margins settle it.
