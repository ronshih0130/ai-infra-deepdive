01

Executive snapshot

為什麼是現在

The AI power crunch stopped being about the grid and became about who can build a power plant fast. Utility interconnection now runs 5–7 years (Columbus, OH quoted at 84 months); a data center must be live in 18–36 months. So hyperscalers are putting generation behind the meter — mostly natural-gas turbines. Turbine OEMs (GE Vernova, Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi) are sold out toward the end of the decade (firm backlog into ~2028–29; slot reservations reaching ~2031). But the binding constraint isn't the turbine assembly floor — it's the hot-section superalloy castings (single-crystal turbine blades & vanes) one layer below, where only ~3 foundries on Earth operate at scale.

Explain it simply · 白話版

The problem: AI data centers need enormous electricity now, but plugging into the public grid takes 5–7 years. So the big tech companies are building their own gas-fired power plants next to the data center. The core machine is a gas turbine — a giant jet-engine-like device that spins a generator.

Where it jams: everyone can see turbines are sold out. The hidden jam is inside the turbine: its hottest spinning parts — the blades — run above the melting point of steel, so they're grown as a single crystal of a nickel "superalloy" and poured in special foundries. Only about three companies worldwide can make these at scale. That casting step is the true chokepoint.

The catch for an amateur: this whole story is now well known — the turbine maker (GE Vernova) and the blade maker (Howmet) have both already doubled or more. The honest edge is (a) a freshly-listed pure-play on the blades — Doncasters (DPC), IPO'd 25-Jun-2026 — that's still un-storied but loss-making/levered, and (b) the cheaper, more-diluted "fast bridge power" names (Caterpillar's engines) the market hasn't fully re-tagged as AI-power.

Single-crystal blade — a turbine blade cast as one continuous metal crystal (no weak grain boundaries) so it survives >1,000 °C. The hardest part to make.
Superalloy — a nickel/cobalt-based metal that keeps its strength when red-hot. The blade's material.
Rhenium — a rare metal (2–6%) added to the best single-crystal alloys to slow "creep." Comes only as a by-product of copper-moly mining → scarce.
Investment casting — the wax-mould pour-and-solidify process that shapes a blade; single-crystal casting is its most demanding form.
Heavy-duty vs aeroderivative — big "frame" turbines (baseload power) vs jet-engine-derived turbines (smaller, faster to deploy, for bridge power).
Behind-the-meter (BTM) — generating power on-site, bypassing the utility grid queue.

From molten metal to a power plant — L3 → L2 → L1

從材料到成品

How the scarce castings build up into a finished on-site power block, and what changes at each step. Value migrates toward the step that is hardest to replicate — the pour, not the assembly.

A single H-class turbine holds hundreds of single-crystal blades; each blade takes weeks to cast and can't be rushed. That's why adding "turbine assembly" capacity doesn't fix the shortage — the pour (L3) is the wall.

05

How each link serves the on-site power paths

部署映射

The same castings feed several deployment paths at different speeds. Prime power (turbines) is the biggest but slowest; bridge power (aeroderivatives + engines) is the "can't-wait" tranche; fuel cells are the escape hatch that skips blades entirely.

Two of three paths route through the casting chokepoint; the fuel-cell path does not. The scarcity is thus real but not absolute — it can be partly relieved by demand defecting to fuel cells (Bloom) and by engines that use fewer single-crystal parts.

08

The chokepoint, scored — and where consensus is wrong

卡點評分

Single-crystal superalloy hot-section castings

Every high-efficiency gas turbine — and every jet engine — needs single-crystal blades that survive above the melting point of their own metal. Only about three foundries on Earth make them at scale: Precision Castparts (captive at Berkshire), Howmet, and Doncasters — together with PCC+Howmet at an estimated ~70–80% of the high-end blade market. Adding turbine-assembly floor space doesn't help: the pour is a decades-refined, low-yield, years-to-qualify process. That is why turbines are sold out to 2030 while OEMs quietly say "expansion willingness of the two blade giants is insufficient."

Variant perception — what the Street believes that we're questioning

Consensus (now fully priced): "buy GE Vernova / Siemens Energy — turbines are sold out through 2030." True, but the OEMs have already re-rated (GEV +59% YTD, Siemens Energy ~4× off its low) and that's the crowded expression.

The disaggregation: the binding constraint isn't the OEM's assembly floor — it's the hot-section casting one layer below, a durable toll that (a) persists no matter which OEM wins and (b) is simultaneously pulled by the commercial-aero engine super-cycle. But the honest catch that keeps us from a slam-dunk: that link has also run (Howmet near an all-time high, ~55× forward earnings), and the scarcest incumbent (PCC) is captive at Berkshire — so the only genuinely-early listed expression is the freshly-IPO'd Doncasters (DPC, 25-Jun-2026), which is a real pure-play but loss-making and levered.

Second disaggregation: "the power bottleneck is bullish gas turbines" under-weights that the urgent tranche is defecting to reciprocating engines (24–30 mo vs turbine 5 yr) and SOFC fuel cells (Bloom, ~90-day installs). Bullish on-site power in aggregate; it spreads the rent and relieves the specific premium-turbine-slot scarcity.

If we're wrong, it's most likely because: (1) low-/no-rhenium single-crystal alloys + additive repair ease the casting choke faster than expected; (2) castings are long-cycle and order-driven — an AI-capex pause hits blades hardest; (3) fuel cells/engines take more share than modeled, capping premium-blade volume; (4) OEMs/PCC expand or in-source faster than believed.

Rent-capture test — margins settle it

Does the scarce-link owner keep the rent, or the buyer? Here — unusually — rent is captured at both the OEM and the casting: GE Vernova Power EBITDA margin +470 bps with prices up 195–300%/3yr, and Howmet at 32% adj EBITDA raising capex/prices. That's genuine toll capture (unlike GOES steel in Run #21, where the material stayed cheap). The pure-play Doncasters, by contrast, still runs a net loss (−$167M on $886M rev) — rent not yet captured, because of leverage. Reciprocating-engine makers (CAT, Cummins) capture volume at competitive margins. So: OEM (GEV) ≈ scaled casting (HWM) > diversified genset (CAT) > pure-play casting (DPC, unproven) > raw material (no listed vehicle). The problem for an amateur: there is no cheap-and-scarce combo — the scarce link is priced (HWM), the cheap link is unproven (DPC), and the diversified link is diluted (CAT).

The escape hatch — why it matters

Bloom Energy (BE) is the disaggregation risk made investable. SOFC power blocks install in ~90 days and use no turbine blades — bypassing the entire chokepoint. Bloom's $25B Brookfield commitment (Jun-2026) and Oracle's up-to-2.8 GW deal mean the "urgent tranche" of on-site power has a real non-turbine path. It's the strongest argument that the blade choke is severe-but-not-absolute (capped at 18, not 20). But BE itself is in euphoria (52-wk $24→$351) and is a binary single-product story — a watchlist tell, not a top-3.
